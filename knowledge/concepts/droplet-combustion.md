# Definition

Droplet combustion is the burning of a liquid fuel droplet, with the fuel evaporating and burning in a flame around it. NASA describes the textbook problem as a spherical droplet surrounded by a spherical flame in an infinite, quiescent atmosphere, which a long-duration, high-quality microgravity environment makes possible because buoyancy no longer distorts the flame.

# NASA Evidence

- NASA states that droplet combustion research has investigated departures from quasi-steady combustion, the transition from diffusive to radiative extinction, liquid-phase transport, the influence of convection on flame dynamics, soot formation and destruction, multi-component fuels, and droplet-droplet interactions and flammability limits.
- For droplets, NASA describes a flammability boundary of ambient oxygen versus droplet diameter, demonstrated in space-based and, to a limited extent, drop-tower tests; larger droplets have lower stretch rates.
- In FLEX, large alkane droplets burned briefly with a hot flame that extinguished by radiative loss, then kept vaporizing, supported by a low-temperature cool flame that formed after visible flame extinction; NASA describes this cool flame as not only unexpected but thought impossible by many in the peer community.
- The ISS supports droplet combustion through the CIR and its MDCA insert (droplets of 1 to 5 mm, 0 to 3 atm, up to 40 percent oxygen).

# Evidence in IGNIS Records

Summary of linked records (see each record for sources):

- FLEX: methanol and heptane droplets in nitrogen- and carbon-dioxide-diluted atmospheres; limiting oxygen index and extinction modes.
- FLEX-2: pure, bi-component and surrogate fuels, droplet pairs and slow convective flow.
- ACME CFI-G: steady spherical cool flames on gaseous-fuel burners, inspired by the droplet cool-flame discovery.

# Fire Safety Relevance

Team interpretation: droplet results give the cleanest microgravity data on liquid-fuel flammability limits and suppressant dilution, and the cool-flame finding shows that a liquid fuel can keep reacting after its visible flame has gone out. Single droplets are idealised; they do not represent pools, sprays or spills directly.
